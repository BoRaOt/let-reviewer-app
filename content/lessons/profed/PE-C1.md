# PE-C1 — The child and adolescent learner: development and learning principles

Section C of the Professional Education outline (30 items, 20% of the part) asks you to use **student-centered, metacognitive, inclusive and developmentally appropriate** pedagogy. It also asks you to read the learning environment, differentiate teaching, and "draw implications of research findings related to child development along biological, cognitive, linguistic, socio-cultural dimensions" [SRC-0001, ProfEd Section C]. Most items are at the *Apply* level (15 of 30), so expect classroom scenarios. Know each theory well enough to recognize it in action.

## 1. Plain explanation

**Development theories** describe how people change.
- **Piaget:** thinking develops in four stages, from learning through the senses to abstract reasoning.
- **Vygotsky:** thinking grows through social interaction. A learner can do more with help than alone (the zone of proximal development), and the helper gradually removes the support (scaffolding).
- **Erikson:** personality develops through eight psychosocial "crises" across the whole lifespan, each resolved toward a strength such as trust or identity.
- **Kohlberg:** moral reasoning moves from avoiding punishment, to caring what others think, to principles like justice.
- **Bronfenbrenner:** the child sits inside nested systems, from family and school out to culture, all moving through time.
- **Freud:** an older theory of psychosexual stages, now widely disputed.

**Learning theories** describe how learning happens.
- **Classical conditioning (Pavlov):** a neutral signal becomes linked to something that already triggers a reflex.
- **Operant conditioning (Skinner, building on Thorndike's law of effect):** consequences change how often a behavior happens.
- **Observational learning (Bandura):** we learn by watching others.
- **Memory (information processing):** information passes through sensory, short-term and long-term memory.
- **Motivation:** it can come from inside (intrinsic) or outside (extrinsic). Maslow's hierarchy puts survival needs at the base and self-actualization at the top.
- **Multiple intelligences (Gardner):** at least eight kinds of intelligence. Educators use it widely, but cognitive psychologists criticize its lack of evidence.

**For the classroom:** a secondary teacher's learners are mostly in Piaget's formal-operational stage and Erikson's identity stage. Lessons can use hypotheticals and abstract reasoning, and should give room for identity questions. Lessons should also be scaffolded, socially rich and fair to diverse learners.

## 2. Testable facts

**Piaget (cognitive stages)**
- Piaget's four stages, with typical ages: **sensorimotor 0–2**, **preoperational 2–6**, **concrete operational 7–11**, **formal operational 12+**. [SRC-0087, 1.3 Table 1.2]
- Sensorimotor: the world is experienced through senses and actions. Key issues: **object permanence**, stranger anxiety. [SRC-0087, 1.3 Table 1.2]
- Preoperational: uses words and images to represent things but lacks logical reasoning. Key issues: **pretend play, egocentrism**, language development. [SRC-0087, 1.3 Table 1.2]
- Concrete operational: understands concrete events and analogies logically and performs arithmetic operations. Key issue: **conservation**. [SRC-0087, 1.3 Table 1.2]
- Formal operational: **abstract reasoning**, abstract logic, moral reasoning. [SRC-0087, 1.3 Table 1.2]

**Vygotsky (sociocultural)**
- Vygotsky emphasized the role of **social interaction** in cognitive development. [SRC-0087, 1.3]
- **Zone of proximal development (ZPD):** concepts just beyond a learner's current ability that they are ready to master with a little help, often from others. [SRC-0087, 1.3]
- **Scaffolding:** support that helps a learner achieve beyond their current level and is **gradually withdrawn** as they become more competent (e.g. training wheels on a bicycle). [SRC-0087, 1.3]

**Erikson (psychosocial stages)**
- Erikson built on Freud's psychosexual theory and proposed a **psychosocial** theory. Each stage poses a "psychosocial crisis" between the individual and society's expectations. [SRC-0087, 1.3]
- Erikson's eight stages and ages: **trust vs mistrust (0–1)**; **autonomy vs shame/doubt (1–3)**; **initiative vs guilt (3–6)**; **industry vs inferiority (7–11)**; **identity vs confusion (12–18)**; **intimacy vs isolation (19–29)**; **generativity vs stagnation (30–64)**; **integrity vs despair (65+)**. [SRC-0087, 1.3 Table 1.1]
- Identity vs confusion (adolescence): experimenting with and developing identity and roles. [SRC-0087, 1.3 Table 1.1]
- Industry vs inferiority (school age): self-confidence when competent, or a sense of inferiority when not. [SRC-0087, 1.3 Table 1.1]

**Kohlberg (moral reasoning)**
- Kohlberg extended Piaget's work on moral reasoning and studied responses to dilemmas such as the **Heinz dilemma**. He cared about the **reasoning**, not the yes/no answer. [SRC-0086, 9.2]
- Three levels: **pre-conventional** (decisions based on consequences to oneself: avoid punishment, be obedient, get a reward); **conventional** (judging by what others think, or by law and order); **post-conventional** (internalized principles such as justice and compassion). [SRC-0087, 8.2]
- Typical timing: pre-conventional before about age 9–10; conventional from early adolescence; post-conventional once formal operational thought is reached, and only a few fully achieve it. [SRC-0086, 9.2; SRC-0087, 8.2]

**Bronfenbrenner (ecological systems)**
- **Microsystem:** settings the person directly experiences, such as family, peers, school and teachers. [SRC-0087, 1.4]
- **Mesosystem:** interactions *between* microsystems, e.g. parent–teacher communication. [SRC-0087, 1.4]
- **Exosystem:** settings that affect the person indirectly, e.g. a parent's workplace policy that changes home life. [SRC-0087, 1.4]
- **Macrosystem:** culture, beliefs, ideologies, laws, economic systems and values. [SRC-0087, 1.4]
- **Chronosystem:** all levels change together through historical time. [SRC-0087, 1.4]

**Freud (psychosexual)**
- Five stages: **oral (0–1)**, **anal (1–3; conflict over toilet training)**, **phallic (3–6)**, **latency (6–12)**, **genital (12+)**. Most of Freud's ideas have not found support in modern research. [SRC-0086, 9.2 and 11.2 Table 11.1]

**Behaviorism and social learning**
- Classical conditioning (Pavlov): an **unconditioned stimulus** (meat powder) produces an **unconditioned response** (salivation). A **neutral stimulus** (a tone) presented just before it comes to trigger the response. [SRC-0086, 6.2]
- Skinner's operant conditioning built on **Thorndike's law of effect**: the consequences of an action alter how often it happens. [SRC-0087, 1.3]
- **Reinforcement increases** a behavior; **punishment decreases** it. **Positive** = something is *added*; **negative** = something is *removed*. [SRC-0086, 6.3 Table 6.2]
- A **variable-ratio** schedule (reinforcement after an unpredictable number of responses, e.g. gambling) gives a high, steady response rate and the **slowest extinction**. [SRC-0086, 6.3]
- **Bandura:** observational learning, i.e. learning by watching others. [SRC-0087, 1.3]

**Intelligence, memory, motivation**
- **Gardner:** at least eight intelligences: linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, spatial, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic. The theory is criticized by cognitive psychologists for lacking empirical evidence, though educators still use it. [SRC-0086, 7.4]
- **Atkinson–Shiffrin (1968)** memory model: information passes through **sensory memory → short-term memory → long-term memory**. Long-term capacity is believed to be unlimited. [SRC-0086, 8.1]
- Short-term memory is a temporary store and is a *component* of working memory; the two terms are not exactly the same. [SRC-0086, 8.1]
- **Intrinsic** motivation comes from internal satisfaction; **extrinsic** motivation is about getting something from others. [SRC-0086, 10.1]
- **Maslow (1943):** physiological needs at the base, then safety, love and belonging, and self-worth (esteem), with **self-actualization** at the top. It can be realized only when lower needs are met. [SRC-0086, 10.1]

**Early years**
- The Early Years Act (RA 10410) recognizes ages **0 to 8** as the first crucial stage of educational development. [SRC-0074, title]

**Motivation and learner differences (second pass, Stage 5; Seifert & Sutton Ch. 4–6)**
- **Intrinsic motivation** comes from within the learner; **extrinsic motivation** is a tendency to direct attention and energy that originates **outside** the learner. [SRC-0109, Ch. 6 (Student motivation)]
- **Self-efficacy** is the belief that you can carry out a specific task or reach a specific goal (like confidence, defined more precisely). Very low self-efficacy can lead to **learned helplessness**, a perceived complete lack of control over mastering a task. [SRC-0109, Ch. 6 (Student motivation)]
- Attributions about success and failure vary in **locus** (internal, e.g. ability, vs external, e.g. an easy test), **stability** (ability is stable; effort is unstable), and **controllability** (effort is controllable) (Weiner). [SRC-0109, Ch. 6 (Student motivation)]
- **Mastery goals** sustain interest in a subject better than **performance goals**; competitiveness of performance goals can produce negative byproducts. [SRC-0109, Ch. 6 (Student motivation)]
- **Self-determination theory:** motivation runs on a continuum from highly extrinsic to highly intrinsic, and depends on three basic needs: **autonomy, competence, relatedness**. [SRC-0109, Ch. 6 (Student motivation)]
- Carole **Ames's TARGET** model integrates motivation ideas: **Task, Authority, Recognition, Grouping, Evaluation, Time**. [SRC-0109, Ch. 6 (Student motivation)]
- **Field-dependent** learners perceive globally; **field-independent** learners prefer analytic work and working alone. The term "learning styles" may imply more consistency across situations than really exists. [SRC-0109, Ch. 4 (Student diversity)]
- The most frequent special need is **learning disabilities**, impairments in specific aspects of learning, especially **reading**; **ADHD** is a problem with **sustaining attention and controlling impulses**. [SRC-0109, Ch. 5 (Students with special educational needs)]
- Inclusion expects teachers to provide **alternative assessment**, a **least restrictive environment**, and to help create **individual educational plans (IEPs)**; in the US the trend accelerated with the **IDEA (1975, amended 2004)**. [SRC-0109, Ch. 5 (Students with special educational needs)]

## 3. Common mistakes

- **Mixing up Piaget and Erikson.** Piaget = *thinking* (cognitive). Erikson = *personality and social* crises. "Object permanence" and "conservation" are Piaget; "trust" and "identity" are Erikson.
- **Stage-age boundaries vary by book.** One OpenStax table gives preoperational as 2–6 and concrete as 7–11; many reviewers say 2–7. Exam items normally test *order and features*, not exact edge ages. Learn the order and the hallmark of each stage.
- **Negative reinforcement is not punishment.** Negative reinforcement *removes* something unpleasant to *increase* a behavior. Punishment *decreases* a behavior.
- **ZPD vs scaffolding.** The ZPD is the *zone*: what a learner can do with help. Scaffolding is the *support* given inside it, which is then withdrawn.
- **Mesosystem vs exosystem.** Mesosystem = two of the child's own settings interacting (home and school). Exosystem = a setting the child is not in that still affects them (a parent's workplace).
- **Kohlberg's conventional level** is about what others think, or rules and laws. Following a principle *against* the law is post-conventional.
- **Freud is not Erikson.** Freud: psychosexual, five stages, ends at genital. Erikson: psychosocial, eight stages, across the lifespan.

## 4. Memory aid

**Piaget: "Smart Penguins Count Fish"** — Sensorimotor, Preoperational, Concrete, Formal.
**Erikson: "TAI-II-GI"** — Trust, Autonomy, Initiative, Industry, Identity, Intimacy, Generativity, Integrity.
**Bronfenbrenner: "My Mom Expects Many Chores"** — Micro, Meso, Exo, Macro, Chrono, from closest to widest, then time.
