**Virtue ethics** takes a **character-centered** approach. Instead of asking "What rule applies?" or "What result is best?", it asks "What would a person of good character do?"

For the ancient Greeks, including **Aristotle**, the aim of life is **eudaimonia**: a happy, flourishing life lived well.

Aristotle described each virtue as a **mean between two vices**: one of **deficiency** and one of **excess**. Courage lies between cowardice, too little, and rashness, too much. Generosity lies between stinginess and wastefulness. The mean is not a mathematical average; it is the right response for the situation.

In *Nicomachean Ethics* Book II, Aristotle says moral virtues come about through **habit**. We become just by doing just acts, and brave by doing brave acts. That makes virtue ethics close to character education: practice good acts until they become second nature.
Memory aid: "Virtue = the golden mean, built by habit, aimed at eudaimonia."
