Communication scholars describe the process with three families of models.

**Linear transmission models** treat communication as a **one-way** flow: a sender encodes a message and sends it through a channel to a receiver. All early models were linear, including **Lasswell's** ("Who says what, in which channel, to whom, with what effect?"), the **Shannon–Weaver** model, which added noise, Gerbner's, and **Berlo's** source–message–channel–receiver model.

**Interaction models** add a **feedback loop**: the receiver decodes the message and responds, so communication goes back and forth, though in turns.

**Transactional models**, such as **Barnlund's** from the 1970s, see both people as sending and receiving **at the same time**, shaping meaning together in context. These are non-linear and multi-directional.

A lecture with no questions fits a linear model; a class discussion where the teacher reads faces and adjusts while speaking fits a transactional one.
Memory aid: "Linear = one-way; Interaction = take turns; Transaction = together at once."
