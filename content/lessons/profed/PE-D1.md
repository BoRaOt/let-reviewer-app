# PE-D1 — Assessment of learning

ProfEd Section D is **22 items** (15%; Remember 3, Understand 3, Apply 12, Analyze 2, Evaluate 1, Create 1). Outcomes: traditional and authentic assessment; formative and summative tools; **rules of test construction**; product and process assessment; **interpreting results**; **basic statistics**; feedback to learners and parents. [SRC-0001, Annex A, ProfEd D] HE-C1 covers formative/summative, performance assessment, rubrics and portfolios; this lesson covers test quality, item writing, statistics and score interpretation.

## 1. Plain explanation

**Quality of an assessment = validity + reliability + absence of bias.** Validity: are the interpretations of results justified (does it measure what it should)? Reliability: is it consistent? Bias: does it distort scores because of gender, ethnicity or class?

**Item types.** **Selected response** (true-false, matching, multiple choice) is objective and reliable to score but cannot measure every goal. **Constructed response** (completion, short answer, essay) reduces guessing and measures complex outcomes but is harder to score reliably.

**Score interpretation.** **Criterion-referenced**: what the learner can do against a standard. **Norm-referenced**: how the learner compares with others.

**Statistics.** Central tendency: **mean, median, mode**. Spread: **range, standard deviation**. In a **normal curve**, about 68% of scores lie within ±1 SD. Standard scores: **z** (mean 0, SD 1), **T** (mean 50, SD 10), **stanine** (mean 5, SD 2).

**Feedback** should be prompt and specific, telling learners what they did well and what to change.

## 2. Testable facts

**Validity, reliability, bias (Seifert & Sutton Ch. 11)**
- High-quality assessment needs **good validity and reliability and absence of bias**. [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Selecting appropriate assessment techniques I: high quality assessments"]
- **Validity** concerns the **interpretations and uses** of results (not the test itself) and is a matter of **degree**. Three sources of evidence: **content** (does it cover the intended content and tasks?), **construct** (does it measure the intended construct, e.g. not English proficiency on a math-reasoning test for recent immigrants?), and **criterion-related/predictive** (does it predict later performance, e.g. admission tests and freshman grades?). [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Validity"]
- **Reliability** is the **consistency** of measurement. It increases with **more items/tasks**, **clear directions and tasks**, and **clear scoring criteria**. Example: one confusing item changes a 5-item score by 20% but a 50-item score by only 2%. [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Reliability"]
- **Bias:** components that distort performance because of personal characteristics such as gender, ethnicity or social class. Two types: **offensiveness** (negative stereotypes, e.g. all doctors men, all nurses women) and **unfair penalization** (items that depend on background experiences some groups lack). Poor performance alone is not bias. [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Absence of bias"]

**Selected response items**
- Selected response items ask learners to **recognize**, not recall; they are **objective** (scoring not influenced by judgment), which raises reliability, but using **only** objective tests can **lower validity** because they do not fit all goals. Assessment must be **aligned to the learning goal** (if the goal is to conduct an experiment, have students conduct one). [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Selected response items"]
- Common item-writing problems: **unclear wording**; **irrelevant cues** (e.g. correct options or true statements are **longer**); **negatives or double negatives**; sentences **copied from the textbook** out of context; **trivial** questions. [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Common problems"]
- **True-false:** suits factual knowledge (vocabulary, formulas, dates, names, terms); efficient and easy to write, but **50% chance of guessing**. [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Strengths and weaknesses"]
- **Matching:** two parallel columns; usually **more options in the second column** so one error does not force another; measures lower-level knowledge (persons–achievements, terms–definitions); hard to make **homogeneous** lists. [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Strengths and weaknesses"]
- **Multiple choice** is the most used objective format: it can assess **higher-level thinking** such as application, reduces guessing (four or five options), and does not need homogeneous material. Three steps: a **clearly stated problem in the stem**, **plausible alternatives**, **no irrelevant clues**. [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Strengths and weaknesses"]
- MCQ errors: the stem does not state the problem (the item becomes a series of true-false statements); options **not grammatically consistent** with the stem; correct answers **clustered in one position** — all positions should be used about equally (but not exactly equally). [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 Table 37]

**Constructed response items**
- Learners **recall and create** answers, so **guessing is reduced**. **Completion** (a statement with a blank) and **short answer** (a question) are answered with a word, phrase, number or symbol. [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Constructed response items", "Completion and short answer"]
- **Extended response (essay):** best for **complex outcomes — integration and application** — and assesses writing; easy to write poorly, hard to write well; good prompts make the task clear, sometimes with **planning notes**. [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Extended response"]
- Scoring essays reliably calls for a **scoring rubric** or point scheme. [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Scoring rubrics"]

**Norm- vs criterion-referenced (Ch. 12)**
- **Criterion-referenced** tests measure performance against a **specific standard** (e.g. "mastered 65% of the grade-level problems"); **norm-referenced** tests report performance **relative to others** in a norm group (e.g. percentile). For planning instruction, criterion-referenced information is usually **more useful**. [SRC-0109, Ch. 12 "Basic concepts"]

**Basic statistics (Ch. 12)**
- **Mean** = sum of scores ÷ number of scores; **median** = the middle score (half above, half below); **mode** = the most frequent score (two modes = **bimodal**). [SRC-0109, Ch. 12 "Central tendency and variability"]
- The **mean is strongly affected by extreme scores (outliers)**; the **median is not**, so with extreme scores the median often better shows the typical score. Example: 4, 18, 18, 19, 19, 19, 19, 19, 20, 20 → mean 17.5, median 19. [SRC-0109, Ch. 12 "Central tendency and variability"]
- **Range** = highest − lowest score; it uses only two scores. **Standard deviation** reflects how much, on average, **all** scores deviate from the mean; **variance** is the mean of the squared deviations (SD is its square root). [SRC-0109, Ch. 12 "Central tendency and variability", "Calculating a standard deviation"]
- A **normal distribution** is bell-shaped and **symmetric**; **mean = median = mode**. [SRC-0109, Ch. 12 "The normal distribution"]
- In a normal distribution, **34%** of scores lie between the mean and +1 SD (and 34% between the mean and −1 SD), so **68%** lie within ±1 SD; about **14%** lie between +1 and +2 SD; about **2%** above +2 SD. The mean is the **50th percentile**; **+1 SD is about the 84th percentile**. [SRC-0109, Ch. 12 "The normal distribution"]
- **z-score = (score − mean) ÷ SD**; mean 0, SD 1. Example: IQ 130 with mean 100, SD 15 → z = 2. **T-score:** mean 50, SD 10 (T 70 = z 2). **Stanine:** nine-point scale, mean 5, SD 2, whole numbers only. [SRC-0109, Ch. 12 "Kinds of test scores"]
- **Grade-equivalent** 3.7 = performance expected of a Grade 3 learner in the 7th month; often misunderstood (a 4th grader scoring 6.0 cannot necessarily do 6th-grade work). [SRC-0109, Ch. 12 "Grade equivalent scores"]
- **Qualitative data** are shown with pie charts and bar graphs; **quantitative** data are counts or measurements. [SRC-0091, 1.2]

**Feedback (Ch. 11)**
- Effective feedback is **prompt** (delay lets misconceptions persist), says clearly **what was done well and what needs changing** (not just "good work, A"), and can use **rubrics**; teachers can lessen the focus on grades by putting the grade **after the comments**. [SRC-0109, Ch. 11 "Providing feedback"]

**Levels of thinking**
- The LET's own TOS uses the revised Bloom levels **Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create**. [SRC-0179, "Cognitive"; SRC-0001, Annex A]

**Second pass (Stage 5): location, shape and relationship**
- A **quartile** splits data into four parts; the **median is the 50th percentile (2nd quartile)** and the **3rd quartile is the 75th percentile**. [SRC-0090, 8.5]
- When a distribution is **skewed to the left**, the mean is often **less than** the median; **skewed to the right**, the mean is often **greater than** the median; in a **symmetrical** distribution mean, median and mode are close or equal. [SRC-0091, 2.6]
- **Correlation coefficients** range from **−1 to 1** (e.g. −1, −0.97, −0.55, −0.03, 0.61, 0.97, 1); values near ±1 show strong linear relationships, values near 0 almost none. [SRC-0090, 8.8]
- **Correlation does not imply causation** — one of the most common statistical fallacies. [SRC-0090, 8.8]

## 3. Common mistakes

- **Validity is about interpretation**, and a test can be reliable but not valid (consistently measuring the wrong thing).
- **Objective ≠ valid.** Objective items are reliable to score but may not fit the goal.
- **Longest option is the answer** is an item-writing flaw (a cue), as are grammatical mismatches and answer-position clustering.
- **Median resists outliers; mean does not.**
- **68% within ±1 SD**, not 50% or 95%. +1 SD ≈ 84th percentile (50 + 34).
- **T = 50 + 10z; stanine mean 5, SD 2.**
- **Criterion-referenced tells what a learner can do**; norm-referenced tells rank.
- *Source caution:* the Seifert & Sutton text contains three slips — Piaget's birth year given as 1880 (it is 1896), z = 2 called the "84th percentile" (z = 1 is), and School A/B SD values that contradict their ranges. Those statements are **not** taught.
