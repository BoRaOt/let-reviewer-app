# GE-09 — Ethics

GenEd unit, **15 items** (10%). Outcomes: decide **what is good for individuals and society**; **reason through moral dilemmas**. [SRC-0001, Annex A, GE] Expect dilemma items asking which theory a reasoning represents.

## 1. Plain explanation

**Facts vs values.** You cannot get an "ought" from an "is" alone (Hume). Assuming you can is the **naturalistic fallacy**.

**Is morality objective?** **Moral realism** says yes; **moral relativism** denies any universal justification for moral beliefs.

**Three main normative theories.**
- **Consequentialism** judges acts by results. **Utilitarianism** (Bentham, Mill): do what produces the **greatest happiness for the greatest number**. Act utilitarians apply this to each act; rule utilitarians use it to choose rules.
- **Deontology** judges acts by **duty**. **Kant**: the **categorical imperative** — act only on maxims you could will as universal law; treat humanity always as an end, never merely as a means. **W. D. Ross**: consider several duties together with consequences.
- **Virtue ethics** asks what a person of good **character** would do. **Aristotle**: virtue is the **mean** between deficiency and excess; the goal of life is **eudaimonia** (a flourishing, happy life); virtues come from **habit**.

**Other voices.** **Care ethics** (Gilligan, Noddings) focuses on relationships and care. **Daoism** (Laozi) and **Mohism** (Mozi) add Chinese perspectives.

**In Philippine schools:** RA 11476 puts **GMRC** in Grades 1–6 and **Values Education** in Grades 7–10.

## 2. Testable facts

**Facts, values and metaethics (OpenStax Introduction to Philosophy)**
- The **naturalistic fallacy** is the error of assuming you can derive **values** (what people **ought** to do) from **facts** (what **is**); it is related to Hume's **is-ought problem**. [SRC-0089, 8.1]
- **Moral relativism** is an **anti-realist** position: it denies that there is an objective or universal justification for moral beliefs. Moral debate challenges **moral realism** by making morality seem subjective. [SRC-0089, 8.3]

**Normative theories**
- There are three main approaches to normative moral theory: **consequentialist, deontological, and virtue ethics**. [SRC-0089, 9.1]
- **Consequentialism:** an action is right when it produces the **greatest good for everyone**. [SRC-0089, 9.2]
- **Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832)** was the first to articulate the **principle of utility**; **John Stuart Mill** formulated the **greatest happiness principle**: actions are right that produce the **greatest happiness for the greatest number**. [SRC-0089, 9.2]
- **Act utilitarians** apply the greatest happiness principle **case by case**; **rule utilitarians** use it to establish a set of **moral rules**. Act utilitarianism can seem to justify actions far beyond ordinary moral standards. [SRC-0089, 9.2]
- **Mohism** (founder **Mozi**) is an early Chinese form of consequentialism. [SRC-0089, 9.2]
- **Deontology** comes from Greek *deon* (duty) + *logos* (study): "the study or science of **duty**"; deontologists do not judge rightness solely by consequences. [SRC-0089, 9.3]
- **Kant:** the only thing of **unconditional value** is a **good will** (the decision to carry out one's moral duties). **Categorical imperatives** are universal laws we must obey **regardless of our desires** (unlike hypothetical imperatives). [SRC-0089, 9.3]
- **Universal law formulation:** "Act only according to that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law." **Humanity formulation:** treat humanity, in yourself and others, "always at the same time as an **end**, never merely as a **means**." [SRC-0089, 9.3]
- **W. D. Ross (1877–1971)**, a pluralist deontologist, held that consequences matter **in light of our duties**; a theory ignoring either duty or consequences "over-simplifies the moral life". [SRC-0089, 9.3]
- **Virtue ethics** takes a **character-centered** approach. For the ancient Greeks (Plato, Aristotle, Stoics, Epicurus) the aim of life was **eudaimonia**, a happy or flourishing life. [SRC-0089, 9.4]
- **Aristotle:** a virtue is the **mean between two vices, deficiency and excess**; moral virtues come about as a result of **habit** (*Nicomachean Ethics*, Book II). [SRC-0089, 9.4]
- **Daoism** began in the Warring States period; **Laozi** (6th century BCE) is its founder; **wu wei** is central to Daoist ethics. [SRC-0089, 9.5]
- **Care ethics:** **Carol Gilligan (1982)** found men often approach moral problems from **justice** and women from **care**; **Nel Noddings**, *Caring* (1984), developed the care perspective. [SRC-0089, 9.6]

**Moral development and values education**
- **Kohlberg** studied the **reasoning** people give to dilemmas such as the **Heinz dilemma**, not their yes/no answers (stages in PE-C1); Gilligan's care perspective responded to his justice focus. [SRC-0086, 9.2]
- **RA 11476 (GMRC and Values Education Act):** GMRC and Values Education **replace Edukasyon sa Pagpapakatao**; **GMRC in Grades 1–6** as a separate subject (integrated in kindergarten); **Values Education in Grades 7–10** as a separate subject, with the same time allotment as other core subjects. [SRC-0038, Sec. 4]
- **GMRC** refers to universally accepted basic social values and etiquette that convey respect to others. [SRC-0038, Sec. 3(c)]

## 3. Common mistakes

- **Utilitarianism counts everyone's happiness**, not just the agent's (that would be egoism).
- **Act vs rule utilitarianism:** "Lying here produces more happiness" is act; "A rule of honesty produces more happiness overall" is rule.
- **Kant: motive and duty, not outcome.** Lying to save a friend is still wrong for Kant because the maxim cannot be universalized.
- **Hypothetical imperative** ("If you want X, do Y") ≠ **categorical** ("Do Y").
- **Aristotle's mean is not "average behavior".** Courage is between cowardice (deficiency) and rashness (excess).
- **Relativism is anti-realist**; realism holds there are objective moral facts.
- **Values Education starts in Grade 7**, GMRC in Grades 1–6.

## 4. Memory aid
